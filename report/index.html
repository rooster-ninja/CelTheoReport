§ I. The Question

Three predictions are set beside the star's place. The first supposes a globe, and traces the ray by Ciddor's index of refraction through the air as the weather balloon found it that night. The second supposes a plane, and takes the angle of the summit by rise over run, without refraction. The third supposes a plane, and allows the star the refraction of the common tables. Each residual is observed less computed: a positive figure means the star stood higher than the hypothesis allows at the instant it vanished. Beside each residual in arc stands its equivalent in seconds of time, being the error of the clock that would close it.

Where the form of the ground along the star's own azimuth is known from survey, the skyline that truly hid the star is used, and not the summit alone. No figure in these pages has been altered or computed afresh; all are transcribed from the reductions.

    Appendix. Of the Method

    The place of the star. Each star's catalogue position, with its proper motion, is carried to the instant of the observation, and its altitude and azimuth are found for the observer and for the summit. The altitude called true is geometric, as it would be seen through a vacuum, reckoned above the observer's horizon on the ellipsoid.

    The ray. For a spherically layered atmosphere the ray obeys Bouguer's law, b = n · r · cos α, in which n is the index of the air, r the distance from the Earth's centre, and α the inclination of the ray to the local level. The quantity b is constant along the ray. The index is Ciddor's of 1996, computed from the temperature, pressure and moisture of each layer. The ray is stepped in segments of metres, at a wavelength of nanometres, to a height of metres, above which the air is taken as void.

    The refraction of the star. The star's apparent place is found by the same AstronomyLive ray trace run backward from its true altitude, by repeating the trace until the ray leaves the air at the star's true direction. That is done at the observer and again at the summit. Bennett's formula of 1982 and the simpler model inside astropy are given beside it for comparison only. The astropy model is not meant for altitudes this low and runs well short.

    The figure of the Earth. The radius used for each line is the radius of curvature of the WGS-84 ellipsoid in the normal section along the azimuth from observer to summit. Distances are geodesics upon the same ellipsoid.

    The frame of the drawings. Every profile is drawn from the foot of the summit, upon the plane tangent there. Both hypotheses place the summit alike. Under the globe the ground and the observer fall away below that plane; under the plane they do not. The fall of the observer is written on each plate.

    The ground. Heights along each line are taken from bare-earth surveys: the 3DEP of the United States at one-third of an arc-second, and the HRDEM of Canada at two metres, with coarser models where these fail. A second surface, which includes the forest, serves only to warn where trees might alter the skyline.
